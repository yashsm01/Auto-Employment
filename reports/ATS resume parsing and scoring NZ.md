# Fix the layout, tailor the content per job

A resume builder for New Zealand job seekers should not maintain a separate file for each applicant tracking system. Every ATS with documented behaviour rewards the same conservative file: single-column, text-based DOCX or PDF, no tables, text boxes, images or header-held contact details, standard headings, and a size under **2.5 MB**, the one numeric parse ceiling that any vendor publishes ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/200989175-Unsuccessful-resume-parse)). What should change per job is the content: the role vocabulary, which experience leads, and the cover letter or form answers. The "ATS score" that consumer tools sell is a keyword-overlap diagnostic with no published formula and no validated pass mark, and most enterprise ranking is opt-in and labels candidates "top fit" rather than rejecting them. The honest product is a coverage view for one job, shown next to a parse preview, with no pass threshold. Much of the evidence is vendor-run or snippet-level, and the NZ-specific ATS evidence is thin, so the design should rest on the conservative format rather than on claims about any one system.

## Parsers reward plain text and punish structure

The parsers that most ATSs embed are commercial engines. Textkernel's platform accepts **more than 70 formats**, including PDF, Word, HTML, RTF and TXT, but scanned PDFs need an optional OCR add-on and "images take considerably longer to parse" ([Textkernel](https://developer.textkernel.com/TKPlatform/master/file-formats)). Sovren's FAQ says the parser handles "any non-image resume" and recommends a practical check: open a suspect PDF, choose "Save as Text", and if the output is garbled the parser will struggle too ([Sovren FAQ](https://kb.bullhorn.com/invenias/Content/Invenias/Topics/parsingTechnicalSpecificationsAndSovrenFAQ.htm)). That test is cheap and vendor-neutral, so the builder should run it on every PDF it exports.

The structural failure list is the most consistent body of evidence. Greenhouse names image-only uploads, graphics and word art, "complex resumes with tables, headers, and footers", columned layouts, and name or contact details inside a header, footer or text box as causes of failed or partial parses, and says a failed parse leaves the candidate's details to be entered by hand ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/200989175-Unsuccessful-resume-parse)). iCIMS rejects a resume whose name appears only in a document header ([iCIMS community](https://community.icims.com/articles/HowTo/Creating-New-Candidates-via-Bulk-File-Transfer-Migrating-Legacy-Resumes-during-Implementation)), and Taleo does not parse image-format resumes ([Oracle](https://docs.oracle.com/en/cloud/saas/readiness/hcm/24a/tale-24a/24A-tbe-wn-f32282.htm)). Workday's admin guide says results "can vary based on resume format and order of words" and recommends no images, while also noting that hidden fields, Languages and Skills are not auto-filled ([Workday](https://doc.workday.com/admin-guide/en-us/human-capital-management/recruiting/candidates/set-up-prospects-and-candidates/hdc1552497830785.html)). SAP's SuccessFactors KBAs report that some mapped fields, such as supervisor and salary, do not populate, and that company names can come back as "UNSPECIFIED" ([SAP KBA 2951470](https://userapps.support.sap.com/sap/support/knowledge/en/2951470), snippet only).

Columns are the hard case. Enhancv's vendor-run test of 17 templates and 357 runs found that word recovery was nearly identical, but under the worst extraction method the section structure stayed intact in **35% of two-column** runs against **60% of one-column** runs ([Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/two-column-resume-ats-test/)). Textkernel's own work concedes that column detection was a known weak point and reports improvement from 62% to 90% of "well-rendered" CVs after a model change, without publishing the method ([Textkernel](https://textkernel.com/learn-support/blog/improving-extraction-from-column-resumes)). On format, the largest comparison is an atsverification sample of about 8,220 self-selected resumes, where 51.8% of DOCX files had no reading problems against 32.4% of PDFs, and 24.1% of PDFs had words glued together against 7.8% of DOCX ([atsverification](https://atsverification.com/blog/pdf-vs-word-for-ats/)). That source is weak, since the author issued a correction retracting an earlier claimed test of named ATSs, so it supports only the directional claim that PDF quality drives failures, not that DOCX is categorically better.

## An ATS score is a consumer diagnostic, not a gate

The scores sold by consumer tools measure overlap with one job description, and none publishes its formula. Jobscan says its Match Rate is "a Jobscan visualization tool, not a score assigned by the employer's ATS," yet its page recommends "at least 75%" without citing any data ([Jobscan](https://www.jobscan.co/resume-score)). Resume Worded's 85+ target and its relevancy score, which weights required qualifications above preferred ones, are likewise undocumented in their weights ([Resume Worded](https://resumeworded.com/score-guide.php)). Teal's score counts job-description keywords present in the resume, colour-coded green, yellow or red, and Teal itself says it is "not to mirror an applicant tracking system score" ([Teal](https://intercom.help/tealhq-9a9274919b13/en/articles/10604396-what-is-job-matching)). No consumer score has been validated against interview or hire outcomes, so the 75, 80 and 85 figures are marketing targets.

Real ATS ranking exists but is configured by the employer. Greenhouse's Talent Matching "does not automatically advance or reject candidates," and recruiters make the decisions ([Greenhouse](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/41396009937307-Talent-Matching)). Lever's Talent Fit labels top-matched candidates and does not reject the rest, and it is enabled per job ([Lever](https://help.lever.co/hc/en-us/articles/26828963215645)). LinkedIn sorts applicants into "Top fit," "Maybe" and "Not a fit" using must-have qualifications and the share of preferred ones met ([LinkedIn](https://business.linkedin.com/au/en/hire/post-jobs/ai-assistant)). Taleo is the clearest knockout mechanism: mandatory disqualification and prescreening questions filter candidates on their answers, not on resume text ([Oracle Taleo](https://docs.oracle.com/en/cloud/saas/taleo-enterprise/21b/otfru/prescreening-candidates.html)). The widely quoted "75% of resumes are rejected by ATS" traces to a 2012 sales pitch with no study behind it ([Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/does-ats-reject-resumes/)). Enhancv's 2025 survey, which is vendor-run and small (25 US recruiters), found that 23 of 25 did not auto-reject on formatting or content, while knockout eligibility rules were used wherever present. The Harvard "Hidden Workers" report is often misquoted; its argument concerns negative filters such as degree and employment-gap rules, not a count of resumes rejected by ATS ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2021/09/new-study-says-hidden-workers-are-being-excluded/)). A newer risk is LLM-screening bias, where simulated LLM screeners shortlisted LLM-written resumes about **23 to 60% more often** than equally qualified human-written ones, a result about machine screeners rather than human recruiters ([arXiv 2509.00462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00462)).

The practical reading is that a recruiter's keyword search, and any knockout questions, matter more than an automated score. On SEEK, recruiters search by keyword against the profile, so profile keywords drive discoverability as much as the uploaded file does ([SEEK NZ](https://www.seek.co.nz/career-advice/5-keywords-employers-are-searching-for-in-your-seek-profile)).

## Per-provider tailoring buys little beyond upload checks and profiles

The documented constraints do not differ enough across providers to justify separate files. Greenhouse accepts DOC, DOCX, PDF, RTF and TXT for upload, with a 100 MB upload ceiling, but parses only up to 2.5 MB ([Greenhouse file types](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/360052218132-Supported-file-types-for-resumes-and-cover-letters)). Workday's accepted formats are not officially documented; blog guides disagree between DOCX and text-based PDF, and their claims are unverified. Lever's format list and whether its application form is pre-filled are also undocumented ([Lever](https://help.lever.co/hc/en-us/articles/20087345054749), snippet only). For the NZ platforms the evidence is weakest. SEEK's and Trade Me's file limits and parsing behaviour were not found in official sources. Trade Me stores up to 100 documents and sometimes requires a cover letter per application, though a community post on that point may be out of date ([Trade Me Jobs](https://help.trademe.co.nz/hc/en-us/articles/360007000152); [Trade Me community](https://help.trademe.co.nz/hc/en-us/community/posts/12205319454479-Option-to-use-CV-uploaded-to-account-but-not-a-cover-letter-but-account-shows-cover-letter-uploaded)). JobAdder, PageUp, SnapHire and Martian Logic returned no parsing or format documentation at all, so any claim about them is unverified.

The per-provider work that the evidence does justify is therefore narrow. The builder should check file type and size at export, keep profile keywords in sync for SEEK and Trade Me, produce a per-job cover letter where a platform needs one, and offer a criteria-response section for NZ public-sector roles. Trade Me's careers advice recommends a simple, two-page layout without multi-column designs ([Trade Me careers](https://www.trademe.co.nz/c/jobs/article/how-long-can-my-cv-be-)), which matches the general consensus.

## New Zealand conventions favour two plain pages and a work-rights line

New Zealand's government career service, Tahatu, is the best current baseline. It advises keeping the CV to **two pages if you can**, and suggests this section order: name and contact, personal statement, key skills and experience, work history, education and qualifications, interests and referees. It asks for plain black font with no images, and says to give month and year for every date because year-only entries "can raise questions about employment gaps" ([Tahatu](https://www.tahatu.govt.nz/work/applying-for-a-job/how-to-write-a-cv)). Recruiter sources agree on two pages and reverse-chronological work history. Hays puts the professional summary at 50 to 150 words and says to quantify achievements ([Hays NZ](https://www.hays.net.nz/career-advice/tips-for-writing-great-resumes-1682)), while Employment Hero NZ says two pages should be enough and recommends Arial or Calibri and no heavy graphics ([Employment Hero NZ](https://employmenthero.com/nz/blog/cv-dos-and-donts/)). Referees are handled with a closing line, "Referees available on request", and names are given when invited to interview, unless the job ad asks for them.

Personal details are narrower than in many other markets. Tahatu gives no advice on age or marital status and says "don't use images" ([Tahatu](https://www.tahatu.govt.nz/work/applying-for-a-job/how-to-write-a-cv)), and the Human Rights Act 1993 covers the hiring process, including advertisements, application forms and interviews ([Human Rights Act 1993](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1993/82/en/latest/)). A builder should therefore omit photo, date of birth, age, gender, marital status, religion and nationality, and should show a suburb or city rather than a full address. The employment-relevant item is work rights. Employers "must check that a job applicant has the legal right to work in New Zealand" through VisaView or document checks ([Employment NZ](https://www.employment.govt.nz/starting-employment/hiring/the-hiring-process); [Immigration NZ](https://www.immigration.govt.nz/work/for-employers/hiring-people-from-overseas/check-if-someone-can-legally-work-for-you/)). No official wording exists for a CV work-rights line, so the builder should state the actual status, such as "NZ citizen", "NZ resident" or the visa type with its expiry date, and should never claim more than the visa allows.

## What the builder should change per job, and how to score honestly

Per job, the builder should change words, not layout. It can copy the ad's role vocabulary into the skills list and bullets, but only where the candidate's real experience supports it; it can move the most relevant roles and achievements to the top; and it can draft a cover letter or criteria response. It should not change job titles, employers or dates, should not add hidden text, and should not repeat keywords. Hidden white text is easy to detect, and recruiters who find it tend to reject the applicant ([Penn West](https://career.pennwest.edu/blog/2022/11/02/i-had-to-reject-the-application-this-recruiter-shared-why-white-wording-isnt-the-resume-hack-people-think-it-is/)). The honest default is to show which ad terms already appear, and to flag terms that are missing but may be true.

The score should be a coverage view, not a grade. It should separate required from preferred skills and report each count, such as "5 of 6 required, 3 of 7 preferred", rather than folding them into one number. Each matched skill should cite the entry and date where it was used, so the candidate can check it, and matching should use an explicit synonym list (for example "AWS" and "Amazon Web Services") with the matched rule visible. The label should be "Keyword and skill coverage for this job," with a line stating that employer systems differ and that the score does not predict an interview. No pass threshold should appear, because none is validated. Alongside the score, the builder should show a plain-text parse preview, since parsing failures are the documented formatting risk and a preview lets the candidate see what the parser sees. This design is closest to Lever's criteria-driven model and Greenhouse's explicit calibration, and it avoids the unvalidated 75-to-85 bands that consumer tools present as pass marks.

## Conclusion: the layout question is settled, the scoring question is not

The layout question is largely settled. The same single-column, text-based, under-2.5 MB file is the only format the documented evidence supports across Greenhouse, Textkernel, Bullhorn, Taleo, iCIMS and Workday, and the failure modes are consistent across engines. Per-provider files would add maintenance without a documented benefit, and multi-column or designed templates carry a measurable parse risk, so the builder should offer few templates and make them all plain.

The unsettled questions are about the NZ platforms and about outcomes. JobAdder, PageUp, SnapHire, SmartRecruiters, SEEK and Trade Me have no verified parsing, file-limit or match documentation, and no study connects any resume score to callbacks. The builder should therefore test its own parse preview on real exports from those platforms, and should describe its coverage score as a tool for checking the match between a resume and an ad, not as a prediction of how an employer's system will rank the candidate.
