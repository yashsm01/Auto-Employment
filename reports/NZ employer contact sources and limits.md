# Automate published employer contacts, not personal lookups

The app can lawfully and automatically find **company-level and advert-level contacts**: the contact named in a job advert, the employer's published careers or HR address, and business phone or email fields on NZBN where the employer has published them. It should show the Companies Register's director names and roles as ownership context, but never use them as contact targets. Named individuals (HR staff, owners, coworkers) should come from the user, not from an automated lookup. Once a tool compiles individuals' work emails at scale, the **Privacy Act 2020** (collection source and purpose rules), the **Unsolicited Electronic Messages Act 2007** (consent test), and the **terms of the platforms involved** (LinkedIn, WhatsApp, finder vendors) all bite. Finder tools are also unreliable: independent accuracy evidence is thin and conflicting, and some vendors have faced regulatory action. WhatsApp contact should stay user-initiated. This analysis rests on secondary sources, because legislation.govt.nz returned HTTP 403 during research, so verify statutory wording before relying on it.

## Public registers yield company contacts, not people contacts

The NZBN register holds 21 primary business data fields, including phone numbers, email addresses, websites, directors and owners, but [email and phone are optional and self-declared](https://nzbn.govt.nz/whats-an-nzbn/primary-business-data), each carrying a stated purpose that may be "general" or "accounts" rather than HR. The notes did not establish which fields are public by default, and no statistic exists for how many NZ SMEs publish an email on NZBN. NZBN's terms say the register **"will not disclose your email address to third parties"** ([NZBN Terms of use](https://nzbn.govt.nz/about-us/terms-of-use)), which makes bulk harvesting of NZBN emails hard to square with the register's own rules. The [NZBN API](https://portal.api.business.govt.nz/api/nzbn) is free, and public searches need only a subscription key, but it publishes **no rate limits, quotas or tiers**, so the reuse and volume terms must be requested before the app is built.

The Companies Register is narrower still. The [Companies Office data page](https://www.companiesoffice.govt.nz/data-services/available-data/business-entity-data) marks address for communication, email and phone as **Restricted**, which means they are not public data. Director names are likely public, but the page does not list directors as a data field, so this remains **unconfirmed until checked on a live record**. Residential addresses are being walled off: the [Companies (Address Information) Amendment Act 2025](https://www.heskethhenry.co.nz/insights-opinion/director-residential-address-protection-bill-is-passed-into-law/) lets directors with serious safety concerns replace their residential address on the public register with an alternative. The Act's fallback commencement date of 18 November 2026 has now passed, but whether the 2026 regulations have been made is unconfirmed. Sole traders and partnerships are not on the Companies Register at all; they appear on NZBN, with restricted contact details. The practical result is that a director's name and role can be shown, but the only contact route is a published address, and using a register entry to contact someone for a different purpose is a purpose-limit problem under the Privacy Act.

## Job-advert contacts are the most defensible channel

The strongest lawful source is the contact an employer publishes **for the purpose of applying to that advertised role**. The [SEEK NZ follow-up guide](https://nz.seek.com/career-advice/article/how-to-follow-up-a-job-application) (search excerpt, not fetched) says the contact name often appears in the advert or in the automated confirmation email, and that the candidate should otherwise call reception and ask who to address. Coverage is patchy in practice: [RNZ reported in August 2024](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/525126/why-are-some-job-ads-so-secretive) that agencies commonly conceal client names, and that about **22% of Trade Me listings came from agencies** in one month. No source quantifies how often NZ adverts name a person with contact details. Scraping SEEK or Trade Me at scale would breach their terms, and the notes did not verify those terms, so the app should take contacts only from adverts the user is viewing, from the user's own application records, or from employer-controlled pages it is permitted to read.

The recruiter and career-adviser consensus fits this scope. [Applying first and then making one targeted follow-up](https://applywave.app/recruiter-contact/nz-post) (a vendor page, low authority) is the common pattern, with a [wait of about a week or two](https://nz.seek.com/career-advice/article/how-to-write-a-follow-up-email-for-a-job-application-templates-and-tips) before one follow-up, and no more than two. The [NZ Herald warns](https://nzherald.co.nz/nz/jobseekers-need-to-address-trust-issues/7335L4QW6COVGBQLDILCYLUPEM) that a hiring manager does not want to hear from a candidate through **six or seven different channels**. For public-sector roles, the advert and [jobs.govt.nz](https://jobs.govt.nz/) process governs, and direct approaches during an open process are likely unwelcome (an inference; no source states it). Where an advert says "apply online only" or "no contact", that is a stop signal. The notes found **no NZ source on WhatsApp or personal social media in recruitment**, so no WhatsApp norm can be presented as sourced.

## Individuals, finder tools and the legal line

The Privacy Act's central question is whether collecting a named person's work contact from public pages is lawful. The OPC's position, as relayed by secondary sources, is that **public availability does not remove data from the Act**, and it has joined a [global statement on data scraping](https://privacy.org.nz/news/statements-media-releases/new-zealand-part-of-global-effort-on-data-scraping). IPP 2 has a publicly-available exception, but an [OPC AI guidance summary](https://www.russellmcveagh.com/insights-news/ai-tools-opc-releases-guidance-for-organisations-using-ai-tools/) warns against relying on it where data may have been obtained unlawfully. Since **1 May 2026**, [IPP 3A](https://www.bellgully.com/insights/preparing-for-ipp-3a-new-requirements-effective-1-may-2026/) requires notice when personal information is collected indirectly, which describes an app that gathers HR or coworker details from third-party sites. The notes are split on whether an individual running a personal tool is an "agency" under s 7, and the statutory text was not retrieved. The working position should be that a tool offered to others, or run by a business, is an agency. That is an inference, not a finding.

The Unsolicited Electronic Messages Act applies only to **commercial** electronic messages. A single application email with a CV and no promotion is probably outside it. The [DIA FAQ](https://dia.govt.nz/Spam-Frequently-Asked-Questions) states that head-hunting is commercial because it markets a service, so any follow-up that pitches the applicant's services, or any recruiter-style outreach, is in scope. For commercial messages, deemed consent requires that the address is conspicuously published by a person in a business capacity and that the message relates to that person's role, and a single correspondence does not reasonably establish consent to promotional email. Unsubscribe facilities must work for 30 days and be honoured within five working days, and the [DIA](https://www.dia.govt.nz/Spam-NZ-Spam-Law-for-Businesses) gives a penalty of up to $500,000 for businesses. [Harvesting addresses with software](https://dia.govt.nz/Spam-Electronic-Address-Harvesting-Software) for commercial messages is prohibited. The notes did not verify an individual penalty figure, so none is given here.

Finder and people-data tools raise a second problem: they often source data from places the individual never gave it to. [Hunter.io](https://help.hunter.io/en/articles/1922677-how-does-hunter-find-the-email-addresses-returned-in-the-domain-search) crawls public pages and guesses address patterns, which is the most transparent method of the six tools reviewed. Accuracy evidence is contested: a competitor's [Anymail benchmark](https://anymailfinder.com/email-finder-benchmark) gave Hunter a 37.6% verified rate, while a separate 200-profile test gave 85% valid. The spread shows that test design drives the result. Apollo's bounce rates in one Reddit test ran around 28-38%, but that is anecdotal. [ZoomInfo's free Community Edition](https://www.zoominfo.com/ce) collects contacts from users' inboxes and signatures, which is the highest-risk model. ZoomInfo has faced [class actions](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/zoominfo-privacy-class-action-gets-early-nod-for-30-million-deal) (a $30 million settlement in Ramos, preliminary approval June 2024), and the Italian data protection authority [fined Lusha EUR 2 million in July 2026](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/42272-data-protection-authority-fined-lusha-systems-eur-2-million-for-unauthorised-processing-of-contact-data), reasoning that continuous profile collection amounts to GDPR monitoring. The French CNIL [dismissed a Lusha case in 2022](https://dataguidance.com/news/france-cnil-finds-case-against-lusha-outside-scope-gdpr) on the opposite view, and neither decision binds NZ. LinkedIn's help pages prohibit third-party scraping and automation and warn of account restriction, and [LinkedIn has litigated the point](https://news.linkedin.com/2025/LinkedInWinsLegalBattleToProtectMemberData) against Proxycurl, which shut down in 2025. The hiQ litigation shows the same pattern. The Ninth Circuit's 2022 ruling found scraping public pages was not a CFAA violation, but it was only an interim decision, and hiQ ended in a consent judgment and permanent injunction, so contract breach is the realistic exposure. "It is public" is not a defence.

## Per-contact-type decisions for the app

The table below sets out what the app can find and show automatically, what needs the user's approval, and what it should leave entirely to the user.

| Contact type | Source | Automatic find and show | Automatic send | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Advert-named contact | The advert or the employer's confirmation email | Yes | Only with user approval, one message per application | Tied to the advertised role. Stop if the advert says no contact. |
| Employer careers or HR address | Employer's own published careers page or contact page | Yes | Only with user approval | Published for recruitment purposes. |
| NZBN business email or phone | NZBN, where published | Yes, if the purpose label is general or accounts, not a personal label | No | Do not bulk-harvest. NZBN terms say emails are not disclosed to third parties. |
| Company name, NZBN, status, registered address | NZBN and Companies Register | Yes | Not applicable | Public data, with restrictions noted above. |
| Director names and roles | Companies Register | Show as context only | No | Confirm on a live record first. Do not treat as a contact target. |
| HR staff, owners and coworkers by name | Finder tools, LinkedIn, directories | No | No | Leave to the user's manual lookup. Compiling these at scale is the highest-risk pattern. |
| Finder-tool or people-data emails | Hunter, Apollo, RocketReach, Lusha, ZoomInfo | No | No | Accuracy is poor and terms and regulatory risk are high. Contributor-style sources are worst. |
| WhatsApp or phone | Any | No | No | User-initiated only. The repo's QR-login WhatsApp service should be checked: an unofficial client falls in the pattern the notes flag as ban and ToS risk. |

## Conclusion

The honest design position is narrower than what the tooling makes possible. The app's lawful automatic reach is limited to contacts that employers publish for recruitment and advert contacts tied to a specific role, with a registry layer that shows company identity and ownership context. Individual lookups are where the Privacy Act's collection and purpose rules, the spam law's consent test and platform terms converge, and where finder tools' accuracy problems make automation a poor trade. The most useful thing the app can do for hiring-manager discovery is to surface the advert contact, the careers page and a checklist of manual steps (company team pages, reception, professional networking), then leave the user to decide who to approach.

The unresolved questions are material. The statutory text of the Privacy Act, UEMA and Fair Trading Act was not retrieved, so the agency question, the IPP 2 and IPP 10 exceptions and the penalties all need checking on legislation.govt.nz. NZBN and Companies Office API rate limits and reuse terms are unpublished, and the NZBN subscription terms should be read before build. Director names being public is unconfirmed. No NZ source addresses WhatsApp in recruitment, and no OPC or DIA guidance addresses job applications specifically. Get legal advice before the app automates any contact step beyond a single user-approved application email.
